# BANT and SPIN Selling

## BANT overview

BANT is a lightweight lead qualification framework originally developed at IBM. BANT stands for Budget, Authority, Need, and Timeline. It is most useful for qualifying inbound leads and transactional or mid-market deals where speed matters more than deep stakeholder mapping.

- Budget: Does the prospect have, or can they obtain, funding for this purchase?
- Authority: Is the contact the decision maker, or can they get access to the decision maker?
- Need: Is there a real business problem that the product solves?
- Timeline: When does the prospect need a solution in place?

A common modern adaptation is to lead with Need instead of Budget, because many buyers do not have a budget allocated until they recognize a problem. Some teams use the ordering NTAB for this reason.

## When to use BANT versus MEDDICC

Use BANT for fast first-pass qualification by SDRs and for smaller deals with one or two decision makers. Use MEDDICC for enterprise opportunities with multiple stakeholders, long sales cycles, and formal procurement processes.

## SPIN Selling overview

SPIN Selling is a questioning methodology developed by Neil Rackham based on research into thousands of sales calls. SPIN stands for Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-payoff questions. The research found that in large sales, successful reps asked more Implication and Need-payoff questions.

### Situation questions

Situation questions gather facts about the buyer's current state, for example "What tools does your team use for forecasting today?" They are necessary but should be kept to a minimum because buyers find too many of them tedious. Do research beforehand to avoid asking what you could have looked up.

### Problem questions

Problem questions uncover difficulties and dissatisfactions, for example "How satisfied are you with the accuracy of your forecasts?" They surface implied needs.

### Implication questions

Implication questions explore the consequences of a problem and make it feel larger and more urgent, for example "What happens to hiring plans when the forecast misses by 20%?" Implication questions are strongly associated with success in large deals.

### Need-payoff questions

Need-payoff questions get the buyer to state the value of solving the problem in their own words, for example "If forecasts were accurate within 5%, how would that help your planning?" When buyers articulate the benefits themselves, they persuade themselves and are better equipped to sell internally.
